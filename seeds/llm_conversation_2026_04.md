Why the hypothesis is reasonable The early-modern European context makes it plausible on priors. Voynich's carbon dating (early 15th century, ~1404–1438) is about 150 years before Dee and Kelley, but the tradition of angelic/received writing didn't start with them — it runs back through Trithemius, the Ars Notoria manuscripts, Hildegard of Bingen's Lingua Ignota (12th century, and notably she also invented a script, the Litterae Ignotae), and various Jewish and Islamic mystical traditions of revealed alphabets. A 15th-century manuscript produced in this mode would not be anomalous; it would be part of a recognizable genre. Hildegard is actually the best precedent — a single author, extensive material, an invented script, claims of divine origin, and a real (if idiosyncratic) semantic system underneath. And Voynich's statistical profile is the thing that makes the "elaborate hoax / meaningless" theory hard to sustain. The mutual information decay, the word-length distribution, the existence of Currier A and B as apparently distinct "dialects," the topic-structured vocabulary clustering (herbal sections use different words than balneological sections) — these are hard to produce by accident or by a hoaxer without a system. Torsten Timm and others have shown you can generate Voynich-like text with a self-citation algorithm, but the process that produces it is itself a kind of generative grammar, which doesn't really dissolve the mystery so much as relocate it. What the angelic-transcription hypothesis specifically predicts This is where you can do real work, because the hypothesis isn't vague — it makes distinctive predictions that differ from both the "natural language in cipher" and the "meaningless hoax" theories. First, it predicts phonotactic leakage from the scribe's native language. Kelley's Enochian has English-like consonant clusters and English-like syllable structure, even though the vocabulary is alien. If Voynich was produced by a 15th-century Central European scribe in a glossographic state, you'd expect the syllable structure, the consonant-vowel ratios, and the permitted clusters to resemble a plausible L1 — likely a Germanic or Romance language, possibly Latin given the scholarly context. Jacques Guy and others have looked at this; Voynichese has unusually rigid syllable structure (words tend to follow a prefix-midfix-suffix template) that doesn't cleanly match any European language's phonotactics. That's actually a point against simple angelic transcription, because you'd expect the scribe's phonological habits to show through. Unless the "template" is the phonological habit, in which case it's informative. Second, it predicts semantic thinness. Received texts, when they do encode meaning, tend to encode less than their length suggests. The writer is producing fluent surface form without the semantic density of composed prose. One way this shows up: high repetition of phrases and high local predictability, combined with weak long-range thematic coherence. Voynich does have very high local repetition — the famous "daiin daiin" sequences, the way words repeat in adjacent positions far more than in natural language. This is genuinely weird and is one of the strongest arguments against Voynich being straightforward natural language. It is consistent with glossographic production, where the writer falls into rhythmic/phonological loops. Samarin documented exactly this in Pentecostal glossolalia — heavy reliance on a small number of phonological templates with combinatorial variation. Third, it predicts topical grounding without lexical grounding. If the scribe was in a visionary state while looking at plants, stars, and bathing women (the three main illustration types), you'd expect section-specific vocabulary — which Voynich has — but you'd not expect those words to decode into plant names, star names, etc., because they were never assigned those meanings in the compositional sense. They're phonological responses to the visual/emotional content of what was being depicted or imagined. This is subtly different from the "it's a pharmacopoeia in cipher" theory, and it predicts that attempts to match Voynich plant-section words to known medieval plant nomenclature should fail — which they largely have. Fourth, and this is the one I'd push hardest on: it predicts single-author structure even across apparent scribal hands. Voynich has at least two scribal hands (Currier identified them) and arguably up to five or eight by more recent analyses (Lisa Fagin Davis's work). The received-transcription hypothesis in its strongest form would need to explain why multiple hands produce mutually consistent text. Options: (a) one visionary dictating to multiple scribes, (b) a school or circle sharing a practice, (c) one primary producer and others copying. Davis's analysis suggests the hands are working collaboratively rather than sequentially, which is compatible with a workshop/circle model — and that's exactly the Dee-Kelley model scaled up, or the Hildegard-and-her-nuns model. What would actually distinguish this from competing theories Here's the test I'd want to run if I were you. Take the Currier A and Currier B subcorpora and treat them as separate writers. The received-transcription hypothesis predicts that the differences between A and B should look like the differences between two people in a shared glossographic tradition — same phonological templates, different surface habits, weak semantic alignment. The natural-language-cipher hypothesis predicts A and B should look like either dialects of one language or two registers of one language — strong semantic alignment under the right mapping. The hoax hypothesis predicts essentially arbitrary differences. Specifically: train embeddings separately on A and B, then try to align them with unsupervised cross-lingual methods. If they align cleanly, it's one system and probably encodes something. If they align partially — function-word-like tokens align but content-word-like tokens don't — that's the fingerprint of a shared phonological/grammatical template with independent "content" generation, which is what received transcription would produce. If they don't align at all, it's two different things. The other test: phonotactic comparison against Hildegard's Lingua Ignota. Lingua Ignota is the closest known analog — a 12th-century invented language/script by a visionary, with surviving glossary (~1000 words glossed into Latin and German). Its phonotactics are known, its relationship to Hildegard's German/Latin L1 is known. If Voynichese's phonotactic profile resembles Lingua Ignota's relationship to its base languages more than it resembles any natural language's profile, that's a meaningful signal. I don't know if this specific comparison has been done rigorously; if it hasn't, it's a real contribution waiting to be made. The honest assessment The angelic-transcription hypothesis explains several Voynich features that the cipher and hoax theories struggle with: the rigid syllable templates, the extreme local repetition, the topic-clustering without decipherable content, the failure of every serious cipher attempt over a century. It struggles with: the multiple scribal hands (explicable but requires auxiliary assumptions), the coherent illustration program (someone planned this manuscript, which fits poorly with pure trance production), and the sheer labor involved (240 folios is a lot for a visionary project, though Hildegard's output was comparable). Where I'd actually land: Voynich is probably not "pure" angelic transcription in the Kelley sense, but it may well be a product of a tradition that used glossographic techniques — a constructed system by someone or some group familiar with ars notoria practices, possibly with a genuine semantic layer for some material (labels, maybe) floating on top of a largely phonologically-generated body text. That would explain why the labels in the astronomical section behave slightly differently from the running text, which has been observed. If you're working on this seriously, the two people whose work you want to engage with are Lisa Fagin Davis (for the paleographic/scribal hand analysis, which constrains any production theory) and Claire Bowern (linguist, has done careful statistical work on Voynichese that's more methodologically sound than most). Bowern in particular has been cautious about overclaiming and her papers are a good model for what defensible analysis looks like in this space. And if you haven't read Samarin's Tongues of Men and Angels cover to cover, do — it's the empirical foundation for any serious glossographic theory of Voynich, and most Voynich researchers haven't touched it. Let me take it seriously, because you're essentially asking: if Voynichese is an obfuscated or degraded version of a natural language whose second-order entropy "should" be 3–4 bits, what transformations would reduce it to ~2, and can any of them be reversed? The framing matters. Voynichese's famously low h2 isn't just low — it's low in a specific way. The conditional entropy drops fast, meaning once you know one character, the next is highly predictable. That's the signature of either (a) a very restrictive phonotactic template, (b) heavy redundancy introduced by a transformation, or (c) a writing system with high grapheme-to-phoneme ratio (lots of characters per underlying sound unit). All three are recoverable-from in principle, but by very different methods. Transformations that lower entropy, and their reversibility Verbose cipher. This is the hypothesis that each plaintext letter is mapped to multiple Voynich characters — so one Latin letter becomes two or three glyphs. This mechanically lowers per-character entropy because the multi-glyph units are internally predictable (once you see the first glyph of a pair, the second is constrained). William Friedman suspected something like this. If Voynichese is a verbose cipher on Latin or a Romance language, the reversal is: find the grouping boundaries, collapse each group to a single unit, then measure entropy. Philip Neal and others have tried variants of this. The tell is that the collapsed corpus should show natural-language entropy (3–4) and natural-language character distributions. Nobody has made this fully work, but nobody has definitively ruled it out either, because the space of possible groupings is large and the grouping might not be positional (it might depend on context). You can test this directly on your corpus. Try every grouping scheme: adjacent pairs, adjacent triples, grouping by glyph class (gallows + following character as a unit, for instance — this is a real Voynichese feature, the "gallows" characters behave structurally like they might be initiators of something). For each scheme, compute h2 on the collapsed sequence. If any scheme jumps h2 from ~2 to ~3.5, you have a candidate. The gallows-as-group-initiator hypothesis is especially worth trying because it's motivated by the paleography, not just statistics. Abjad-style vowel suppression, inverted. Semitic scripts have lower entropy than you'd expect because they mostly write consonants and let readers supply vowels. But Voynichese's problem is the opposite — it has too many characters for too little information. So imagine the inverse: a script that writes vowels and consonants and something else (tone? length? a morphological marker?), inflating the character count per phoneme. If each underlying phoneme is represented by two Voynich characters on average, you'd roughly halve the entropy, landing around where Voynich sits. Reversal: identify which glyph classes are "redundant" markers vs. phonemic. The statistical signature would be that certain glyphs appear in highly predictable positions relative to others — which, again, Voynichese shows. Syllabic padding or templating. This is the one I find most compelling given what we know about Voynichese's rigid word structure. If the underlying language is being forced into a CVC or (prefix)(root)(suffix) template where the template positions constrain what can appear, you get exactly the entropy collapse you see. The slots are doing work that reduces uncertainty. Reversal: identify the template, strip the padding characters, see what's left. Stolfi's prefix-midfix-suffix decomposition of Voynich words is essentially this analysis, and it's suggestive — when you decompose words into their slot contents, the distributions in each slot are more language-like than the whole-word distributions. Whether what remains after stripping has entropy ~3–4 is a computation I don't know has been done cleanly, and it's exactly the kind of thing you could do. Abbreviation/nomina sacra-style compression. Medieval Latin manuscripts used heavy abbreviation systems — suspension, contraction, special sigla for common words. If Voynichese is a language written in a heavily abbreviated system where abbreviations are graphically regular, you'd get lower character-level entropy because the abbreviation symbols are predictable in context. Reversal requires a dictionary of abbreviations, which you don't have, but you can infer candidates: look for glyphs or glyph sequences that appear in positions where a natural language would have common function words (sentence-initial, between content words), and hypothesize them as abbreviated et, est, que, etc. This is the approach Bax tried, with mixed results. Substitution plus transposition within words. If words are real but their letters are scrambled by a fixed rule, the unigram frequencies are preserved but the bigram frequencies are destroyed and replaced by the transposition pattern. This would lower h2 if the transposition rule is regular, because the scrambling introduces its own predictability. Reversal: try candidate unscrambling rules and check if any produce bigrams matching a known language. This is computationally cheap for short rules (reverse the word, swap positions 1 and 2, etc.). The diagnostic you actually want Here's what I'd do in your shoes. Rather than trying transformations blindly, first characterize where the entropy is being lost. Compute positional entropy — h2 conditioned on position within the word. If entropy is uniformly ~2 across all positions, you're looking at a systemic transformation (verbose cipher, substitution). If entropy varies dramatically by position — high in the "root" slot and low in the prefix/suffix slots — you're looking at templating, and the real language is in the high-entropy slots. This single analysis rules out big chunks of the hypothesis space. Then compute entropy conditioned on word length. Natural languages have roughly constant per-character entropy across word lengths. If Voynich's entropy drops sharply for longer words, that's evidence of templating or padding — longer words are longer because they've accumulated more predictable affixal material, not more information. If it stays flat, templating is less likely. Third: entropy on first characters of words only, ignoring everything else. This strips away within-word predictability and tells you about the lexicon-level entropy. If word-initial entropy is ~3–4 while overall h2 is ~2, the "language" is real at the lexical level and the low h2 is a within-word artifact — which is exactly what you'd expect from a templated or verbose-encoded natural language, and exactly not what you'd expect from pure glossographia, which should be low-entropy all the way down. This last test is, I think, the cleanest discriminator between your angelic-transcription hypothesis and the obfuscated-natural-language hypothesis. Pure received writing should have low entropy at every level because the generative process is phonological template-matching without a real lexicon behind it. An obfuscated natural language should have natural-language-like entropy at some level of analysis, even if it's buried — the information has to be somewhere if it exists at all. What this means for your broader project If you run these diagnostics and find that Voynichese has natural-language-like entropy at the word-initial or root-slot level, then the glossographic hypothesis weakens considerably and you should pivot toward "obfuscated/templated natural language" as your working theory. If entropy stays ~2 at every level of analysis, the glossographic hypothesis gets stronger, because then there's nowhere for the missing information to hide — the text genuinely doesn't encode as much as its length suggests, which is the defining property of received writing. The really interesting outcome, and I think the most likely one based on what I know about Voynichese, is mixed: some structural levels show natural-language entropy and others don't. That would suggest a hybrid — real linguistic material (maybe labels, maybe section headers, maybe the roots) embedded in a matrix of phonologically-generated template material. Which is, not coincidentally, how actual medieval magical/visionary texts often work. The Ars Notoria has real Latin prayers interleaved with verba ignota, the "unknown words" that were the operative magical content. Voynich could be structurally similar: a real substrate carrying some information, wrapped in generated material that carries little or none. If that's what's going on, then "bringing the entropy up to 3–4" isn't a single operation — it's identifying which parts of the text carry information and analyzing only those, while recognizing the rest as structured noise. The question stops being "how do we decode Voynich" and becomes "which 20% of Voynich is the signal." Have you looked at whether the entropy differs between the label text (the short annotations next to illustrations) and the running body text?
