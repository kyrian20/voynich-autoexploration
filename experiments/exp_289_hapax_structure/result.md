# Experiment exp_289: Hapax Internal Structure

**Date:** 2026-04-11
**Hypothesis:** H495 — Hapax legomena (words appearing only once) follow the same onset+suffix combinatorics as frequent words. If they do, a productive morphological system generates them. If hapaxes show structural irregularities (unusual onset distribution, rare/missing suffixes, longer midfixes), this suggests a different generation mechanism.
**Status:** pre-registered

## Hypothesis

If VMS words are generated by a productive morphological system (any onset × any midfix × any suffix), hapax words should have the same onset class distribution, suffix distribution, and midfix length profile as frequent words — hapaxes are rare because their specific combination hasn't recurred, not because they're structurally different. Under a template/hoax model, hapaxes might be filler words with atypical structure.

## Method

- Corpus: eva_zl3b.txt, both A and B dialects, body text only (P* loci)
- Define hapax: word type appearing exactly once in the full corpus (not just in one dialect)
- Define frequent: word type appearing ≥ 5 times (moderate frequency), ≥ 20 times (high frequency)
- Onset extraction: ONSET_ORDER from exp_257/288
- Suffix extraction: same SUFFIX_LIST from exp_288
- Metrics: (a) onset class distribution per frequency tier, (b) suffix distribution per frequency tier, (c) midfix length distribution per frequency tier, (d) word length distribution per frequency tier, (e) zero-midfix rate per tier
- Comparison: KL divergence between hapax and frequent-word distributions
- Control: random-text control — generate words from Voynich character frequency distribution; compute hapax rate and structure for this control
- Dialect: separate A and B analysis

## Expected Outcomes

| Theory | Predicted result |
|--------|-----------------|
| Productive morphological system | Hapaxes and frequent words have same onset/suffix distributions; KL divergence is low; hapax length distribution shifts slightly right (more complex forms less repeated) |
| Template/glossographic generation | Hapaxes use same template structure; KL divergence is low; hapax rate is mechanically high due to random template filling |
| Random/hoax | Hapaxes have atypical structure (longer, more unusual onset/suffix combos); KL divergence is higher |

**This experiment is an UNFAIR test of the hypothesis if:** Hapaxes are more common at corpus edges (beginning/end of sections) and these sections have different onset distributions — this would conflate structural with positional effects. Check: does hapax onset distribution change if we restrict to mid-section text?

## Result

Hapax words are structurally DIFFERENT from frequent words. The midfix length gap is 7× (hapax MLen≈4.0, frequent MLen≈0.55 in both dialects). Hapaxes almost never have zero-midfix (1% vs 66-71% for frequent words). The onset distribution KL divergence is 0.94–1.62 bits (Z>444 vs shuffled null). Hapaxes are depleted in the high-frequency sparse-template onsets (ch, d, qok) and enriched in vowel/complex-body onsets (y, o, l, other). The frequent word suffix distribution is dominated by canonical paradigm suffixes (ol, aiin, edy); hapaxes have substantially more unrecognized endings (17.8–23.6% no suffix vs 1.5–5.0% for frequent words).

## Interpretation

**Hapax words are NOT just rare instances of the same morphological paradigm.** They are a structurally distinct word class: internally complex words with long midfixes. Frequent words are predominantly "template words" — onset+suffix with zero or minimal midfix (qokeedy, chedy, daiin, chol). Hapaxes are "vocabulary-rich" words with extended internal character sequences.

**This reveals a two-tier vocabulary structure:**
- **Tier 1 — Template words (frequent):** Short (WLen≈4.5–5.0), onset+suffix direct (ZeroMid=66–71%), high-frequency. These are the grammatical/functional tokens that drive the positional grammar. They're the words the positional grammar rules are written FOR.
- **Tier 2 — Vocabulary words (hapax):** Long (WLen≈7.0–7.2), internally complex (MLen≈3.9–4.2), appear once. They carry the "content" of the writing — whether actual semantic content or generated by some combinatorial process with many interior possibilities.

**Why this matters for generation models:**
- The Mode B simulation generates most words as onset+suffix (low-midfix). The over-produced vocabulary (F280: simulation 2.2× VMS) happens because even adding midfix at p_mf=0.20, the simulation doesn't generate the RIGHT distribution of long-midfix hapaxes. VMS hapaxes appear to come from a different generation process than frequent words.
- The hapax onset distribution (enriched in y/o/l) suggests hapaxes are NOT uniformly drawn from the same onset distribution as frequent words. The generation mechanism would need onset-dependent hapax rates.

**The 0.233–0.215 bit suffix entropy uplift in hapaxes** is consistent with the two-tier structure: hapaxes use slightly more diverse suffixes (including more unrecognized endings) while frequent words concentrate into 4-5 canonical suffix forms.

**Null model rejection:** KL divergence of 1.6 bits at Z=476 means hapax and frequent onset distributions differ to an extreme degree. This is not a sampling artifact.

**Structural explanation:** The frequent words are structurally SHORT because they are the onset+suffix templates (e.g., daiin=5 chars, chedy=5 chars, qokeedy=7 chars). The hapaxes are long because they contain character sequences that don't fit the standard templates — they have unique interior content. Whether this interior content encodes information (natural language morpheme combinations) or is a copying artifact (unique character combinations from random modification) cannot be determined from this measurement alone.

## Limitations

- Hapax frequency is computed globally across A and B dialects. A word appearing once in A and once in B counts as freq=2 (not hapax). This is conservative — some words counted as non-hapax might be sectionally unique.
- The null model shuffles word-frequency assignments; this correctly tests whether onset class and frequency are independent. However, the shuffled null ignores word-length correlation with frequency (shorter words tend to be more frequent in any language). The observed KL is partly driven by word-length effects (long words = long midfix; long words are less frequent). The structural finding (midfix length 7× difference) is not confounded, but the KL value may be partly a length artifact.
- The "no recognized suffix" category (17.8–23.6% of hapaxes) may indicate genuine unusual word endings, or may indicate suffix extraction failure for long words where the suffix is buried deeper.
- The analysis combines tokens from all positions (LF, MID, LL). Hapaxes may be concentrated at specific line positions where unusual words appear more often (e.g., rare word types at LL).

## Self-Audit

**Target-awareness:** No VMS target metric was used to calibrate any parameter. Exploratory measurement. No target-awareness issues.

**Feature-destroying implementation:** The midfix extraction uses longest-match suffix stripping. For very long words (WLen=7-9), the suffix may not be at the correct boundary, inflating midfix length. However, this applies to all frequency tiers proportionally, so the tier comparison is valid. The "no suffix" cases (17-24% of hapaxes) are genuine long words without recognized terminals.

**Extrapolation:** No claims beyond the measured distributions. The "two-tier vocabulary structure" is a descriptive observation, not a claim about generation mechanisms.

**Negative-result scope:** Positive result (hapaxes are structurally different). N/A.

**Control behavior:** Null model (shuffled frequencies) produces KL ≈ 0.006–0.010 bits, as expected (shuffled null should produce near-zero KL). Observed KL is 160–270× the null mean. No unexpected control behavior.
