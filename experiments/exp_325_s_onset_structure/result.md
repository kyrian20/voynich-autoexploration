# exp_325: s-Onset Internal Structure (H524)

**Date:** 2026-04-11
**Hypothesis:** H524 — Is s-onset structurally parallel to d-onset (both 'a'-primary at B0, both potentially aiin-dominated)?
**Result:** H524 CONFIRMED — s-onset and d-onset ARE STRUCTURAL PARALLELS sharing the same body vocabulary. Top s-onset words: saiin(20.4%), sain(10.9%), sar(8.8%), sol(8.0%), sal(7.3%) match the d-onset paradigm (daiin=22.6%, dar=15.9%, dal=9.9%, dain=7.4%, dol=4.2%) with identical bodies (aiin/ain/ar/al/ol/air/or). 10/20 top s-words have d-analogues in the top-20 d-words. The s/d word pair inventory constitutes "onset-substitution doublets" — the same body vocabulary with different onset characters. DIALECT DIFFERENCE: B-s emphasizes -ain/-aiin (high 'a' at B0=62.8%); A-s shows more 'o'-body forms (sor=7.2%, soiin=3.1%, B0='a'=37.1%), following the broader A-dialect 'o'-body preference. Cross-dialect s-onset Jaccard = 0.095 (very low type-level overlap despite structural similarity).

## Findings

### Finding 1: s-onset = d-onset paradigm with different onset character

s-onset and d-onset share the same body vocabulary (aiin, ain, ar, al, ol, air, or, am) with different onset characters ('s' vs 'd'). This is not partial overlap — the 7 most frequent s-words all have d-analogues (s→d substitution gives words that exist in the d-onset corpus), and the 7 most frequent d-words all have s-analogues (d→s substitution gives existing s-onset words).

This constitutes "onset substitution doublets": s and d are interchangeable onsets for the same body inventory. The VMS writing system appears to have a rule that the {d, s} onset class can precede any member of the body vocabulary {aiin, ain, ar, al, ol, air, or, am}. The onset character ('d' or 's') is the only differentiator.

### Finding 2: s-onset is low-frequency (137 B / 97 A) with same body as d-onset (674 B / 732 A)

d-onset is ~5× more frequent than s-onset in both dialects. Despite this frequency difference, they share the same paradigm structure. s-onset does not have a specialized vocabulary — it draws from the same body inventory as d-onset but less frequently.

This is analogous to how y-onset (y+gallows) is structurally a compound of two other onset systems (y + {k,t,p,f}). s-onset may be structurally: s = d-substitute-in-specific-contexts, or s may mark a different grammatical role for the same semantic content.

### Finding 3: A-dialect s vs d divergence in frequency ratios

B-dialect: d/s = 674/137 = 4.9×; A-dialect: d/s = 732/97 = 7.5×. In A-dialect, s-onset is relatively less frequent compared to d-onset than in B-dialect. A-dialect concentrates s-onset into fewer, less frequent tokens. Correspondingly, A-dialect daiin is even more dominant (35.0% of A-d vs 22.6% of B-d tokens), while A saiin is less concentrated (14.4% vs B 20.4%). A-dialect appears to have a stronger "d-onset only" template for the aiin body forms.

### Finding 4: s-onset 'o'-body in A consistent with A-dialect 'o'-preference

A-dialect: sor=7.2%, soinn=3.1%, so=2.1% — 'o'-body forms are more prominent in A than B (B: sor=2.9%, soinn=1.5%). This follows the same pattern seen for ch/sh: A-dialect uses 'o'-body forms where B uses 'a'- or 'e'-body forms. For s-onset, B uses 'a' (B0='a'=62.8%) while A uses 'a' less exclusively (B0='a'=37.1%) and more 'o' (B0='o'=24.1%).

## Interpretation

**s and d are onset-substitution doublets:** The VMS writing system uses the onset characters 's' and 'd' to produce words from the same body inventory (aiin/ain/ar/al/ol/air/or). The two onset classes are structural parallels with different onset characters. This is consistent with a morphological paradigm where the onset-class grammar defines the BODY VOCABULARY, and multiple onset characters can introduce the same body set.

**Why s is less frequent than d:** The frequency ratio (d≈5× more than s) suggests d is the "default" onset for the aiin-body paradigm while s is a less-common alternative. The bigram matrix (exp_324) shows d→d enriched (1.54×) and d as a target from both y→d (1.37×) and s→d (1.62×) — d-onset attracts succeeding d-onset words AND s-onset precedes d-onset. This suggests a content-word sequence pattern: s-onset and d-onset function in the same grammatical position, with s sometimes preceding d.

## Limitations

- Small N for s-onset (B: 137, A: 97); per-word type estimates are noisy at N=1-2
- The 10 s-specific forms (solshey, soinn etc.) that lack d-analogues need investigation — they may represent compound words where 's' is embedded in a longer string, not a true s-onset word
- Cross-dialect Jaccard=0.095 may partly reflect sample size differences (smaller A-s sample misses rare shared types)
