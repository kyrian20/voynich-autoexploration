## Findings

### Finding 1: A-Dialect Gallows→sh Is a Multi-Position Rule (E=1.42-1.72× at LF→pos1, pos1→pos2, MID_inner)

In A-dialect continuation lines, a gallows-onset word elevates sh at the NEXT position regardless of where in the line it occurs: LF→pos1 (E=1.72×), pos1→pos2 (E=1.46×), MID_inner (E=1.42×). The rule does not weaken substantially moving from LF→pos1 to MID_inner. This means the gallows→sh bigram is a general A-dialect positional rule, not specific to the line-initial transition.

### Finding 2: B-Dialect Gallows→sh Is Positionally Specific to LF→pos1 (E=1.22× → 0.78× Reversal)

In B-dialect, the gallows→sh rule is strong at LF→pos1 (E=1.22×) but reverses at pos1→pos2 (E=0.78×, gallows SUPPRESSES sh). At MID_inner it is neutral (E=1.00×). This means the B-dialect gallows→sh rule is a LF-position effect, not a general word-pair bigram. The mechanism may be: gallows LF causes line-initial sh enrichment (positional grammar), whereas in mid-line transitions gallows do not systematically predict sh.

### Finding 3: Pre-LL→LL Is Gallows Anti-sh in A-Dialect (E=0.28×)

In A-dialect, after a gallows-onset word, the next word (LL) is almost never sh-onset: 1.2% vs 4.3% non-gallows (E=0.28×). This is a strong anti-sh constraint at the pre-LL→LL transition in A. The LL position itself has elevated sh generally (daiin, specific LL formulas), but when the pre-LL word is gallows-onset, sh is strongly suppressed. B-dialect shows this less strongly (E=0.85×, directional only).

### Finding 4: Gallows→anti-ch in B-Dialect Is Position-Specific (pos1 and pre-LL but NOT MID_inner)

B-dialect ch depletion after gallows: strong at LF→pos1 (E=0.63×) and pos1→pos2 (E=0.62×), present at pre-LL→LL (E=0.55×), but absent at MID_inner (E=1.14×, slight elevation). The anti-ch rule is confined to line-boundary-adjacent positions (beginning of line and pre-terminal). A-dialect shows no systematic gallows→anti-ch in MID_inner either (E=1.22×, slight ch elevation).

### Finding 5: p-Onset and f-Onset Are the sh-Elevating Gallows in Both Dialects; k-Onset Is the ch-Elevating Gallows

Across all positions combined: A p→sh=23.0%, f→sh=27.8% (strong sh induction); k→sh=13.8%, t→sh=12.9% (moderate). In contrast, A k→ch=22.8%, t→ch=20.7% (k and t induce ch more than sh). The ch/sh ratio after each gallows onset: f=0.40 (most sh-biased), p=0.71 (sh-biased), t=1.61, k=1.65 (ch-biased). This means the gallows "class" is internally divided: {p, f} → sh preference; {k, t} → ch preference. B-dialect similarly: p→ch/sh=0.49 (p strongly sh-biased), f=2.00 (f ch-biased in B). A/B diverge on f-onset: A f→sh-biased, B f→ch-biased.

### Finding 6: B s-Onset Has High sh% at Next Position (19.1%)

The per-onset table reveals B s-onset→sh=19.1% (all positions), which is higher than the gallows-class average (12.8%) and comparable to p-onset (18.5%). A s-onset→sh=13.1% (above A gallows average 14.9% for all positions, similar). This suggests that s-onset (a grammar-word class) also predicts sh at the following position, extending the "sh-inducing" onset set beyond the gallows class.

## Summary

H567 PARTIALLY CONFIRMED with dialect divergence. **A-dialect**: gallows→sh is a multi-position rule (E=1.42-1.72×) across LF, pos1, and MID positions, but specifically suppressed at pre-LL→LL (E=0.28×). **B-dialect**: gallows→sh is positionally specific to LF→pos1 (E=1.22×), reverses at pos1→pos2 (E=0.78×), and is neutral in MID_inner (E=1.00×). The gallows→anti-ch rule in B is also position-specific: present near line edges (pos1, pos1→pos2, pre-LL→LL) but absent in MID_inner. An internal split within the gallows class: {p, f} are sh-inducing; {k, t} are ch-inducing in A-dialect.
